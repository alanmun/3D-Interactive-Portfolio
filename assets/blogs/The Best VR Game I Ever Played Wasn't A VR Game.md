I've been playing Cyberpunk 2077 a lot recently. It's actually my second time playing. The first time was your conventional flat-screen play through on a 2K monitor, high but not maxed graphics. Great experience, paradigm setting visuals and vibes, a real seminal work that would go on to directly inspire culturally on a global scale (hello, cybertruck). I never planned to play through the game a second time, but seeing clips of Luke Ross's REAL VR mod which supported Cyberpunk 2077 amongst other games had sparked serious excitement in me. The clips looked really cool, and if there was a world to virtually enter, it was Cyberpunk 2077! So, after a LOT of configuring, a GPU upgrade (3070 Ti --> 4090), and many hours, I had gotten the setup working! Well, sort of.

I say sort of because the experience was rough- rare crashes, camera glitches due to being in VR, a serious effort just to hit 80fps/80hz refresh, just above the bare minimum to prevent VR nausea. *None of that mattered though* because it felt incredible to just sit in a car and be next to Dexter DeShawn as he introduced me to how shit really goes down in the Night City underground. Or the feeling of sitting at a diner with Goro Takemura, our booth leather seats flexing and crackling as we recline back, slurping down syn-coffee as we plotted with each other in whispers amidst the civilians of Night City, worried that any one of them could be Arasaka spies. I was smitten with moments like this- truly nothing in VR felt more immersive than these experiences I got from this mod. I would even go as far as saying it gave me more enjoyment than even Half Life: Alyx, a game that most consider to be the first and only AAA VR title.

## My scoliosis functioning as a canary in the coal mine and conventional ideas of what makes for immersion in VR
Some readers who are well versed in VR and know how the Luke Ross mod works might take pause with what I just said. They'd point out how the mod is played using a standard two-handed controller, abandoning all motion controls, no usage of legs, just seated gameplay. Aiming occurs through your right stick and your head, something dis-affectionately referred to as "gunhead". A lot of VR enthusiasts take issue with this mod and deride it for these reasons but my conviction is that they are mistaken on what *really* matters when immersing yourself in a new world when you play a game.

Games do need to be fun and immersive, VR or not, its true. However, what gets discounted in conversations online is that a pre-requisite for fun and immersion is comfort and convenience! Half Life: Alyx was played in 45 minute chunks because... I kept getting VR sick consistently by 45 minutes. My girlfriend never took to VR despite my many attempts and guidance on how to get your "VR legs", the sickness from it was ultimately a blocker to her adoption. This is and continues to be the case for many others. I never wanted to give up, because when it worked, *it worked*. It was *magical*. I couldn't stop chasing that dragon. But conventional immersive VR experiences like Pavlov VR (basically Counter Strike but in virtual reality for the uninitiated) demonstrated repeatedly the limits of what I could enjoy. The game has you standing still for long periods of time, in hunched positions as you are scoped in, watching a chokepoint for enemies. It's physically uncomfortable after just 15 minutes of play. I'm in my 20s. I'm sure kids don't feel this pain the way I do, and people who don't have scoliosis likewise can shrug it off more. But, people get older. Those kids will stop being kids one day. And my experience 15 minutes in with scoliosis is akin to someone else's same poor experience, just reached at maybe the 30 or 45 minute mark of standing up in strange unergonomic poses to meet whatever virtual criteria they need to meet to throw the grenade or snipe the guy or save the village. So I know I'm not a true outlier. And all I could think of was how the game's focus on immersion was actually to its detriment for most gamers. Scoliosis just made me notice these problems faster than the average VR enthusiast. 

It is my belief that trying to make your game more immersive/add value to it through the development and inclusion of motion controls, standing room scale play, leg tracking, etc is a misplacement of most developer effort. This is something that became wholly clear to me after playing a "traditional" (flat-screen) game like Cyberpunk in VR. I just booted the game up, grabbed my controller, got comfy, and with all the distractions and discomfort gone, my Valve Index headset's high FOV, 6 DoF, and incredible speakers carried the show and breathed new life into a story I had already seen before.

![pasted image 20260405141640](/assets/blogs/attachments/Pasted%20image%2020260405141640.png)
*This is what the 6 degrees of freedom are, btw. In seated VR games like Cyberpunk, the top three, also referred to via "3DoF", are what you mostly notice since you aren't moving around in your room, but the bottom three also come into play a tiny bit and are what make headsets "6 DoF".*

Being able to stand up and swing a saber or something and your physical actions interact with the virtual world is cool, as a first-time-foray experience. But that's it really, its more of a gimmick than the way I typically prefer to play games as a seasoned gamer. 

I asked myself: Why did I find it so hard to keep playing VR even though I had so much passion for VR? Why did I still prefer to play normal games on my monitor? Why had I allowed my headset to collect dust? I didn't find the answers until I had installed this mod. VR gaming has, in my experience forgotten about the convenience + comfort players were used to in the pursuit of immersion-maxxing, ironically making those same games less fun and leaving the player less wanting to immerse because they were dizzy, nauseous, sweaty, tired of moving around, or their back hurt after just 30 minutes of play. 

> And what about convenience? In flat-screen play, gamers simply grab their controller, ensure its charged, and then press a button to launch the game. In VR, we have to:
> 1. Set up the VR harness. For some people they have to go fetch the left and right controllers + the headset, for others they have to mangle a pulley system or otherwise fix the cables in their wired headset.
> 2. If wireless, they have to make sure 2 controllers and one headset are charged for every play session
> 3. Clear out space. Likely your room setup is not conducive normally for VR play, and you have to move a bunch of things out of the way.
> 4. Put the headset on, grab each controller. Realize you forgot to click something on your computer and so you waddle over to your desk to carefully set down each controller without being able to see, then with your hands free you take your headset back off, so you can dismiss a popup or whatever. Repeat and put your headset and controllers back on again


VR doesn't achieve the most worthwhile parts of immersion by making players stand up for hours and swing to use a hammer or smashing your motion controllers into each other as you desperately plead with the game to recognize that you are trying to perform a gun reload. No, it's better accomplished through the ol' reliable means that largely existed before VR, and then augmenting that experience with the power of the headset while adding little to no additional friction in the process to play a game. Good world design, intricate lore. Not more crap to attach to your body. Surround sound (that good VR headset speakers can provide) that lets you hear someone approaching from behind me and to the right, before I even look around. 
It's things like: 
- Being able to physically look left to make eye contact with the character you were listening to, who now sounds like they're about to start sobbing. 
- Peeking with your head off of the roof of some megastructure in Kabuki to see if you really are about to jump off a building with nothing to break your fall. 
- Slowing down your vehicle and turning your head to peer out the side window to read the strange futuristic ads that have nothing to do with the current mission just because you can.  
This is how immersion is chiefly accomplished and simply donning the headset let me experience it in a way that made me feel like I was really there! Not being expected to stand upright for over an hour. Not leg motion support. There is no need, nor desire, to control my katana with motion and being left drenched in sweat by the end of every session. Again, definitely cool at first, annoying when you're past the novelty phase. I realized these parts of VR were more of a gimmick and stood in the way of actual enjoyment as a member of the more hardcore gaming demographic. The kind that wants more than a 15 minute cardio-intensive arcade experience. 

Maybe in 2077 technology will reach a point where we can just put on an ultra-lightweight 150 gram braindance wreath and get full leg tracking, toe tracking, gastric system tracking so the game can sync up with your bowel movements or whatever the immersion-fiends you see online in VR communities want these days. But for most gamers like me, the real magic lies in just putting on a decent quality headset that makes us feel like we've left our bedrooms and really entered the game's world.
